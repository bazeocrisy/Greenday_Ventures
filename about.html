About

A firm built around long-term ownership.

Greenday Venture works with owners of established home-service businesses who are thinking carefully about what comes next for the company they built.

Why Greenday Venture exists

Many owners of well-run trade businesses reach a point where the question is no longer how to grow, but who should carry the work forward. The options presented to them are often built around speed and price. Fewer are built around what happens after the closing.

Greenday Venture was founded to provide established business owners with a thoughtful path toward long-term ownership transition. The intent is straightforward: buy good companies, keep them running well, and treat the people inside them as the reason the business is worth buying at all.

How we think about ownership

A change in ownership is a change for everyone who depends on the company. Technicians want to know whether the schedule and the pay stay steady. Customers want to know whether the same crew shows up. Family members want to know what the name still means.

Greenday Venture is focused on long-term ownership rather than preparing a business for a quick resale. Improvements, when they come, are meant to make the work easier to sustain, not to strip the company down.

A business owner is not merely selling an asset. The owner may be deciding what happens to decades of work, employees, customers, family history, and reputation.

Respect for what already works

An established company carries knowledge that does not appear on a balance sheet: how jobs get scheduled, which customers to call personally, which technician to send on the difficult call. Understanding that comes first. Changing it, if ever, comes much later.

A Note From Jermane

Why I started Greenday Venture.

If you own a home-service company, you have probably had the call. Someone you have never met, asking whether you have thought about selling, wanting a number by the end of the week. I understand why owners stop answering those calls.

I started Greenday Venture because I think that approach has it backwards. A plumbing company, an HVAC company, an electrical contractor — these are not spreadsheets. They are twenty or thirty years of someone's decisions. Which technician to hire. Which customer to keep even though they are difficult. Which winter to get through without laying anyone off. None of that shows up in a valuation, and all of it is the reason the business is worth buying.

My intention is to be the kind of buyer I would want if the company were mine. That means listening first. Before I have a view about what should change, I want to understand how the work actually gets done, who the people are, what the customers expect, and what the owner is trying to protect. Some of what makes a company work is not written down anywhere. If you arrive with a plan already made, you will break it without noticing.

I also think the people around a transition deserve more attention than they usually get. When an owner sells, the technician who has been there fifteen years finds out something about his own future. So does the dispatcher who knows every customer by name. So does the owner's family, who have lived alongside this business for as long as it has existed. I believe those realities belong in the conversation early, not in a memo after closing.

I want to be straight with you about where Greenday Venture is. This is a focused firm, deliberately built around a single sector, and early in its growth. I believe trust should be earned through direct communication, thoughtful preparation and honest answers rather than exaggerated claims. What I offer is a direct counterpart who answers questions plainly, moves at a pace that does not put the business at risk, and tells you early if something is not a fit rather than spending months of your time.

I chose home-service businesses deliberately. They are essential in a way that is easy to overlook until your heat fails in January or a pipe opens up behind a wall. They are usually local, usually built by hand over decades, and usually run by someone who knows the trade rather than someone who studied it. When one of those companies changes hands badly, a community loses something real. When it changes hands well, the work continues and the people who do it keep their footing.

I want owners to feel comfortable asking hard questions. How will you finance this. Who actually makes decisions afterward. What happens to my crew. What happens to my name. Those are the right questions, and I would rather answer them in the first conversation than have you wonder about them at the closing table. We have published the questions we think you should be asking anyone who approaches you, including us.

Every situation deserves to be evaluated on its own facts. I would rather learn how your business actually works before suggesting what your transition should look like. Some owners want to be finished the day it closes. Others want to keep doing the parts they enjoy for another few years. Both are reasonable, and the structure should follow the person rather than the other way around.

My goal is simple. I want to buy good companies from people who care what happens to them next, and I want the owner to feel, a year later, that they chose well. If you are thinking about what comes next — even if that is several years away — I would welcome the conversation.

Jermane LambFounder, Greenday Venture
